# Technical writing rules

Distilled from Google's Technical Writing One (https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one),
grouped as the course groups them. The examples are ours.

## Words
- Define a new or unfamiliar term at first use, in plain words.
- Use one term for one thing, everywhere. Do not switch between "run", "job" and "task" for the
  same object. Spell out an acronym once; skip it if it appears only once or twice.
- Replace an ambiguous pronoun ("it", "this", "they") with the noun when two things could be meant.

## Sentences
- Prefer active voice: name who does what. Passive voice hides the actor and adds words.
- Pick a precise verb over a weak verb plus a noun ("decide", not "make a decision").
- Keep sentences short, but keep a claim and its reason connected. Split a sentence only when it
  needs a second read.
- Cut words that add nothing ("in order to" -> "to", "it is important to note that" -> delete).
- Replace idioms and figures of speech; readers outside your culture or language miss them.

## Lists and tables
- Numbered list when order matters; bulleted list when it does not.
- Keep list items parallel: same grammatical form, same kind of thing.
- Introduce a list or table with a sentence that says what it shows.

## Paragraphs
- Open each paragraph with a sentence that states its point.
- One topic per paragraph. If you write "also" or "another thing", consider a new paragraph.
- Answer what, why and how for the reader, in that order of need.

## Audience
- Write for a named reader: what they know, what they need, what they will do next.
- Watch the curse of knowledge: you skip steps that feel obvious to you. Have someone without your
  context read it.

## Documents
- State the key point at the start.
- State the scope (what the document covers and does not) and the audience.
- Break long topics into sections a reader can skip to.

## Punctuation
- Our house rule: no em dashes. Use a comma, a period, a colon before a list, or rewrite.
- A semicolon joins two closely related full sentences; usually two sentences read better.
